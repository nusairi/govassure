Beginner path

Identity and the devices people use

Two of the first building blocks are who is asking and what they are asking from.

Identity and access is about four plain questions: how do we set up and change someone's access, how do we check who they are, how do we check they are allowed to do this, and how do we later review that we got those three right? In many modern services, identity is more important than an old “inside the office network” boundary, because people connect from all over the place.

Devices — laptops, phones, servers and other equipment — have to be trustworthy too. A strong login does not help much if it is coming from a computer that is out of date or no longer under the organisation's control.

You do not need to know this yet: the identity video is the longest in the series (just over half an hour). You do not need to remember every tool it mentions. Aim to leave with the four questions above, and the idea that access should be taken away when it is no longer needed.

Videos to watch

  • YouTube · 31:15

    Cybersecurity Architecture: Who Are You? Identity and Access Management (opens in a new tab)

    Sets out the four questions — set up access, prove who someone is, check what they may do, and review it later. Useful later when you look at the Corner's Zero trust access pattern.

    What this video covers

    • Identity is treated as a new kind of perimeter: you cannot rely on the old idea of a safe inside and a dangerous outside, so you need to know who is asking and what they are allowed to do.
    • A simple four-part model is used: administration (setting up and changing access), authentication (who are you?), authorisation (are you allowed to do this?), and audit (checking that the first three were done correctly).
    • Identities live in directories. Different groups of people — staff, suppliers, the public — often need different access, and those records have to be kept in step across systems.
    • Later sections cover how people prove who they are, including multi-factor checks and single sign-on, and how access is reviewed over time, including privileged accounts.
    • Beginners do not need to memorise product names. The ideas to keep are: know the types of user, give only the access that is justified, prove who someone is with more than a password where it matters, and remove access when it is no longer needed.

  • YouTube · 14:22

    Cybersecurity Architecture: Endpoints Are the IT Front Door - Guard Them (opens in a new tab)

    Explains why the device matters as well as the person, and why an organisation needs a clear way to manage laptops, phones and other equipment — including when people use their own devices.

    What this video covers

    • Strong identity checks are not enough if the device they come from cannot be trusted. An endpoint is any device at the edge of the system: laptops, phones, servers, and other connected equipment.
    • Typical practice often uses different management tools for servers, desktops and phones, and sometimes leaves smaller devices unmanaged. Better practice is to know what you have and manage it consistently.
    • Endpoint management is used to apply policy: patches, a reasonable password or lock, and software that looks for malware or suspicious behaviour on the device.
    • People sometimes bring their own devices, and also their own tools or cloud services. An organisation needs a clear approach, not an accidental one.
    • For a first watch, the question to take away is: do we know which devices will touch this service, and can we tell if one of them is out of date or no longer under our control?

What GOV.UK recommends

Official guidance treats identity and the device as two halves of the same problem.

  • NCSC 10 Steps: Identity and access management (opens in a new tab)

    Covers proving who someone is, giving only the access they need, and reviewing that access over time — the same four questions as this step, in official language.

  • NCSC device security guidance (opens in a new tab)

    Official advice on keeping laptops, phones and other devices in a known, trustworthy state, including when people use different kinds of equipment.

  • NCSC zero trust architecture design principles (opens in a new tab)

    These principles say you should not trust a request just because it comes from inside the network. You still check the user, the device and the access each time.

Check your understanding. A service has strong logins, but staff can sign in from any personal laptop, including ones the organisation has never seen. What is missing?

Show a good answer

The team has thought about identity, but not about the device. They need to know which devices will touch the service, and have a way to tell whether those devices are up to date and still under some kind of control.
