What a security architect does

A security architect helps a team design a service so it is safe enough for the people who use it and the information it holds, without making the service hard to use. In UK government you usually sit with the rest of the delivery team, alongside the service owner, technical architects, developers and the people who own the risk.

In practice the job comes down to four things, which this Corner already uses as its starting point:

  • Understand what the service does, what data it holds and what could go wrong.
  • Design security into the service from the start, reusing patterns and platforms that already work.
  • Explain risks in plain English so the right person can make a clear decision.
  • Keep it secure as the service changes, and help close it down safely at the end.

You do not need to build every control yourself. Specialists will implement firewalls, identity systems or encryption. Your job is to see the whole picture, ask how the design might fail, and make sure the important questions are asked early — not after everything is already built.

    A plain comparison between planning a building and planning a system. The useful takeaway is the habit: understand how the service will work, then ask how it might fail, and get involved from the start rather than being asked to make it secure at the end.

    What this video covers

    • A security architect is compared carefully with a building architect: stakeholders say what they need, someone draws the plan, and specialists build it. The security architect should be thinking on the plan, not trying to do every specialist's job.
    • The distinctive habit is this: understand how the service will work, and also how it might fail, then add protections — for example proving who a user is, checking the device, dividing the network, or protecting data.
    • Simple diagrams that show who is involved and how the parts connect are treated as planning tools. Frameworks are offered as a checklist, not a substitute for thinking.
    • Best practice is to involve the security architect from the start, not to call them in after the design is finished and ask them to make it secure.
    • The rest of the series is framed as seven domains: identity, endpoints, networks, applications, data, then detection and response.

What GOV.UK recommends

These official pages describe the role, and the standard a public service is expected to meet.

  • Security architect in the Government Digital and Data Profession Capability Framework (opens in a new tab)

    The official description of the role in government, including what people at each level are expected to do. Useful so you can see the job in government language, not just ours.

  • Service Standard point 9: Create a secure service which protects users' privacy (opens in a new tab)

    The Service Standard says a public service must be designed so it is secure and protects users' privacy, and that this is considered throughout delivery, not only at the end.

  • Government Functional Standard GovS 007: Security (opens in a new tab)

    The government's security functional standard sets out what good security looks like across government organisations. You do not need to learn it by heart; it is the backdrop that departmental security policies sit under.

Show a good answer

They also ask how the service might fail, and what should be in place before that happens. A typical design explains how the parts fit together. A security architect uses that picture to look for weak points, and to help the team fix them early.
