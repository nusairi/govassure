What you will learn

By the end of this path you will be able to:

  • explain what a security architect does, in plain English, and how the role sits with the rest of a delivery team
  • use a few ideas that turn up in every phase, including defence in depth, least privilege, and confidentiality, integrity and availability
  • ask the first questions about identity, devices, networks and data
  • start a first threat-model sketch, and see how official UK guidance talks about the same work
  • place those ideas on the five-phase service lifecycle, including detection and response once a service is live
  • complete a first exercise using templates that already live in this Corner

How to use this path

Open the steps in order, or jump to the one you need. Each page has a short looping explainer, a little reading, official UK government or NCSC guidance, and one or two videos you can open when you have time. Tick “mark as done” on a step and it will show as done here. Ticks are saved on this device only. Nothing is sent to us.

The videos are from a public cybersecurity architecture series on the IBM Technology YouTube channel. They open in a new tab. We have written our own short summaries. Under each video link you will also find a short “What this video covers” explainer and a simple graphic, in our own words. If a video or animation feels like too much, use the pause control, or skip on and come back later. You will not be behind.

You do not need to know everything. The aim is a confident first pass, not a qualification.

Words you will see

A short glossary in plain English. You do not need to memorise it. Come back when a word turns up.

Availability
The service is there when the people who are allowed to use it need it.
Confidentiality
Information is only available to the people who should see it.
Defence in depth
Using more than one control, so that one failure does not open the whole service.
Encryption
Scrambling information so that only someone with the right key can read it.
Endpoint
A device at the edge of the system, such as a laptop, phone or server.
Identity and access management (IAM)
The work of checking who someone is, and what they are allowed to do.
Integrity
Being able to trust that information has not been quietly changed.
Least privilege
Giving people and systems only the access they need, only for as long as they need it.
Multi-factor authentication (MFA)
Proving who you are with more than one check, such as a password plus a code from your phone.
Risk
The chance that something bad happens, and how serious it would be if it did.
Secure by design
Building security in from the start, rather than adding it after the service is already built.
SIEM
A way of bringing security logs and alerts from many systems into one place so someone can look at them. The letters stand for security information and event management.
Threat
A way that something bad could happen to the service, its users or its data.
Vulnerability
A weakness that could be used to cause harm, such as a software defect or a misconfiguration.
Zero trust
An approach that does not assume someone is safe just because they are “inside” the network. You still check who they are, what they can do, and the state of their device.
