Step 1 of 7

What a security architect does

A security architect helps a team design a service so it is safe enough for the people who use it and the information it holds, without making the service hard to use. In UK government you usually sit with the rest of the delivery team, alongside the service owner, technical architects, developers and the people who own the risk.

In practice the job comes down to four things, which this Corner already uses as its starting point:

  • Understand what the service does, what data it holds and what could go wrong.
  • Design security into the service from the start, reusing patterns and platforms that already work.
  • Explain risks in plain English so the right person can make a clear decision.
  • Keep it secure as the service changes, and help close it down safely at the end.

You do not need to build every control yourself. Specialists will implement firewalls, identity systems or encryption. Your job is to see the whole picture, ask how the design might fail, and make sure the important questions are asked early — not after everything is already built.

Step 2 of 7

Ideas that turn up everywhere

A few ideas come up again and again, in every phase of a service. You do not need a long list of jargon. If you can use these in a conversation, you already have a solid start.

  • Defence in depth means not relying on a single lock. If one control fails, another should still stand.
  • Least privilege means giving people and systems only the access they need, only for as long as they need it.
  • Secure by design means building security in from the first conversation, not bolting it on at the end.
  • Keep it simple means the safe way should be the easy way. If the official path is too hard, people will go around it.
  • Confidentiality, integrity and availability are a short checklist: who should see this, how would we know if it was changed, and will it still be there when people need it?

One idea to avoid: hoping a system is safe because nobody knows how it works. Hiding the details is not the same as making something strong.

That is hoping for safety through hiding how something works. A better approach is to use well-understood controls, give people only the access they need, and not rely on a single lock. The secret, if there is one, should be a key or password — not the design itself.

Step 3 of 7

Identity and the devices people use

Two of the first building blocks are who is asking and what they are asking from.

Identity and access is about four plain questions: how do we set up and change someone’s access, how do we check who they are, how do we check they are allowed to do this, and how do we later review that we got those three right? In many modern services, identity is more important than an old “inside the office network” boundary, because people connect from all over the place.

Devices — laptops, phones, servers and other equipment — have to be trustworthy too. A strong login does not help much if it is coming from a computer that is out of date or no longer under the organisation’s control.

You do not need to know this yet: the identity video is the longest in the series (just over half an hour). You do not need to remember every tool it mentions. Aim to leave with the four questions above, and the idea that access should be taken away when it is no longer needed.

The team has thought about identity, but not about the device. They need to know which devices will touch the service, and have a way to tell whether those devices are up to date and still under some kind of control.

Step 4 of 7

Networks and data

The other two building blocks you will hear about constantly are networks and data.

A network is how users, staff and other systems reach the service. A common approach is to divide it on purpose, so that a problem in one place cannot immediately reach everything else, and to allow only the traffic you mean to allow. The same idea applies when a service runs in the cloud. This series does not have a separate cloud video; when you are ready, use the Corner’s Secure cloud landing zone pattern.

Data is often the reason the service exists, and the thing you most need to protect. Start by writing down what you hold, why you hold it, and how sensitive it is. Then you can talk about protection, such as limiting who can see it and encrypting it when that is needed, and about how you would recover if it was lost.

You do not need to know this yet: the networks video is long and names a lot of products and patterns. Skip the product names. Keep the picture of dividing the network and only allowing the traffic you intend.

Step 5 of 7

Thinking about risk and threats

Risk, in plain English, is the chance that something bad happens, and how bad it would be. A threat is a way that bad thing could happen — a mistake, a weak design, a supplier problem, or someone trying to break in.

You do not need a special personality for this. You need a habit: look at the service, ask “what could go wrong?”, decide which of those things matter most, and write that down so the person who owns the risk can make a decision. That is what the Corner’s threat model and risk register are for.

Software is one common source of problems. Defects are normal. The longer they sit unnoticed, the more they cost to fix, and the more likely they are to become something an attacker can use. It is much cheaper to think about this while the service is being designed than after it is already live.

  • YouTube · 16:36

    Cybersecurity Architecture: Application Security (opens in a new tab)

    Why waiting until the end to think about software security is a poor plan, and how teams can build checks into design, coding and testing instead of throwing work “over the wall”. You do not need the testing acronyms on a first watch.

Check your understanding. When is the most useful time to start asking “what could go wrong?” with a new service, and why?

Show a good answer

As early as you can, while the design is still easy to change. Finding a problem in discovery or alpha is cheaper and safer than finding it after the service is already built or live.

Step 6 of 7

How this fits the five-phase lifecycle

UK public services are usually designed in five phases. This Corner already has a lifecycle map for each one, with what the architect does, the documents to start, and the questions to ask before moving on. The ideas from this path sit on that map; they do not replace it.

  • 1 Discovery

    Understand the problem before anyone designs a solution. This is when you ask what the service does, what data it will hold, and what could go wrong. The confidentiality, integrity and availability checklist from step 2 is especially useful here.

  • 2 Alpha

    Try different ways of building the service and pick the safest one that works. This is where identity, devices, networks and the “design it in from the start” habit earn their keep.

  • 3 Beta

    Build the real service and prove it is secure enough to go live. Threat modelling, security testing and a clear design all belong here.

  • 4 Live

    Keep the service secure while it runs and changes. Prevention is not enough: you also need to notice problems and respond to them. The two videos below are for this phase. You can leave them until you are comfortable with the earlier steps.

  • 5 Retirement

    Close the service down without leaving data or access behind. The data questions from step 4 come back here: what do we still hold, and how will we dispose of it safely?

Check your understanding. A team has locked down logins and encrypted their data, but nobody looks at logs and there is no plan for what to do if something gets through. Which lifecycle phase is under-prepared, and what is missing?

Show a good answer

Live. They have put effort into prevention, but not into detection or response. They still need to decide what will be logged, who will look at it, and how the team will react if something goes wrong.

Step 7 of 7

Your first exercise, and where to go next

Pick a service you know, even a very small or imagined one — a form that collects an email address, a booking page, a staff dashboard. Then fill in the Corner’s data and sensitivity summary for it. Write down what data it holds, why, how sensitive it is, and where it lives. Keep classification wording plain. You are practising the habit, not writing a formal assessment.

If that feels comfortable, add a first sketch in the threat model: three or four things that could go wrong, and what you would do about the most important ones. You can save your answers in the browser and come back to them. Nothing is uploaded.

Words you will see

A short glossary in plain English. You do not need to memorise it. Come back when a word turns up.

Availability
The service is there when the people who are allowed to use it need it.
Confidentiality
Information is only available to the people who should see it.
Defence in depth
Using more than one control, so that one failure does not open the whole service.
Encryption
Scrambling information so that only someone with the right key can read it.
Endpoint
A device at the edge of the system, such as a laptop, phone or server.
Identity and access management (IAM)
The work of checking who someone is, and what they are allowed to do.
Integrity
Being able to trust that information has not been quietly changed.
Least privilege
Giving people and systems only the access they need, only for as long as they need it.
Multi-factor authentication (MFA)
Proving who you are with more than one check, such as a password plus a code from your phone.
Risk
The chance that something bad happens, and how serious it would be if it did.
Secure by design
Building security in from the start, rather than adding it after the service is already built.
SIEM
A way of bringing security logs and alerts from many systems into one place so someone can look at them. The letters stand for security information and event management.
Threat
A way that something bad could happen to the service, its users or its data.
Vulnerability
A weakness that could be used to cause harm, such as a software defect or a misconfiguration.
Zero trust
An approach that does not assume someone is safe just because they are “inside” the network. You still check who they are, what they can do, and the state of their device.
