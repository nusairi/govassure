Passwords, 2-step checks and accounts

Most of the time a website checks who you are with a password. The trouble is that passwords get guessed, reused and stolen. The answer is to use more than one kind of proof: something you know, something you have and something you are.

The NCSC's advice is simple. Your email account is the key to all your others, because password resets are sent there, so give it a strong password that you use nowhere else. Make passwords long rather than complicated: joining three random words is a good method. Use a password manager so you do not have to remember them all. And turn on 2-step verification (also called two-factor or multi-factor authentication) for your important accounts.

Passkeys are a newer way to sign in that replace the password with something stored on your device. The NCSC has a topic page on them in the box below.

Try it for yourself

  • Open your email account's security settings. Check that its password is not used anywhere else, and look for 2-step verification. If it is off, switch it on. It takes about five minutes.

    Cybersecurity Assets, Network Threats & Vulnerabilities: Authentication, authorisation and accounting (opens in a new tab)

    How systems decide who you are and what you may do. Some of the later material (sign-in standards and tokens) is technical, so it is fine to stop once the three kinds of proof make sense.

    What this video covers

    • Access control answers three questions: who are you (authentication), what are you allowed to do (authorisation), and what did you do (accounting, covered in the next video).
    • There are three kinds of proof: something you know, such as a password; something you have, such as a one-time code sent to your phone; and something you are, such as a fingerprint or face.
    • Single sign-on lets one login open many services. It is convenient, but risky if it relies on one proof only. Adding a second kind of proof, called multi-factor authentication, makes it far safer.
    • Authorisation should give people only the access their role needs, and only for as long as it is needed. Splitting jobs between people stops one person from both doing and approving the same thing.
    • Sign-in methods that send passwords openly are weak; modern sites use secure web connections, and "sign in with" buttons share limited access rather than your password. Keep extra checks switched on for those accounts.

Check your understanding. Why does the NCSC ask you to give your email account a password you use nowhere else, even before your other accounts?

Show a good answer

Because password reset links are sent to your email. Anyone who controls your email can reset the passwords on your other accounts, so it is the key to all of them. A separate, strong password, plus 2-step verification, protects that key.
