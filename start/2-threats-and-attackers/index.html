Threats, attackers and risk

People who cause harm online are called threat actors. They range from criminals after money, to people who want to disrupt a service, to staff or suppliers who simply make a mistake. Mistakes matter as much as malice: the first video explains that many "inside" threats are accidents.

A handy way to keep four words straight. An asset is something that matters to you. A threat is something that could harm it. A vulnerability is a weakness a threat could use. A risk is how likely, and how bad, the harm would be. You only have a risk where a threat and a weakness meet.

Most attacks are not clever. The NCSC's Cyber Essentials pages describe most attacks as basic, the digital equivalent of a thief trying your front door to see whether it is unlocked. That is good news: basic protections stop most of them.

Try it for yourself

  • Pick one thing you own online. Write down one threat, one weakness and the risk where they meet, then the cheapest fix. Example: threat, a scam email; weakness, the same password on several sites; risk, a stranger gets into your email; fix, a separate strong password for your email.

Videos to watch

These are clips from longer free videos. Each link starts at the right moment, and you are welcome to watch only that part.

  • YouTube · starts at 26:14 · about 10 minutes · part of a video that runs 1 hour 14 minutes

    Cybersecurity for Beginners: History of security (opens in a new tab)

    A tour of early viruses, worms and scams, and why they still matter. It helps you see that today's attacks are usually variations of old ones.

    What this video covers

    • Many of today's attacks are variations of old ones. Looking back at the 1980s and 1990s explains why there are so many security jobs now.
    • A virus attaches itself to programs or documents and spreads; a worm spreads on its own. Today the general word for both is malware: software designed to harm devices or networks.
    • Two early cases: a 1986 virus meant to track copied software, which spread round the world on shared disks, and a 1988 worm released while measuring the size of the internet, which kept reinstalling itself until computers crashed. Neither was meant to do that much harm.
    • A later email worm called LoveLetter looked like a message from a friend, mailed itself to everyone in the address book and infected tens of millions of computers. It is described as the first example of social engineering: tricking people rather than breaking machines.
    • A 2017 breach at a US credit-reporting company exposed the records of well over a hundred million people, partly because known weaknesses had not been fixed. The large settlement showed businesses that prevention is cheaper than the clean-up.

  • YouTube · starts at 13:55 · about 14 minutes · part of a video that runs 1 hour 27 minutes

    How To Manage Security Risks & Threats: Navigate threats, risks and vulnerabilities (opens in a new tab)

    Defines four words you will hear constantly, then shows what an attack costs an organisation and ends with a risk-management cycle used by professionals.

    What this video covers

    • An asset is anything of value, from buildings and laptops to customer details. A threat is anything that could harm an asset, such as a phishing email. A vulnerability is a weakness a threat can use, such as out-of-date software or a weak password. A risk is how likely and how bad the harm could be.
    • Both a threat and a weakness have to be present for there to be a risk. People can be a weakness too, which is why teaching people to spot a phishing email is a good start.
    • Risk is often rated low, medium or high, depending on the value of the asset. Public website content is low; information protected by law or containing personal details is high.
    • Ransomware locks an organisation's data until it pays for the key. The clip also explains the surface, deep and dark layers of the web, and the three costs of an attack: money, identity theft and damage to reputation.
    • It ends with a seven-step risk cycle from a US standards body: prepare, categorise, select, implement, assess, authorise and monitor. Treat it as a glimpse of how professionals work, not something to memorise.

What GOV.UK recommends

Official UK sources show how common attacks are, how basic most of them are, and what a minimum level of protection looks like.

  • NCSC: Cyber Essentials (opens in a new tab)

    The government-backed minimum standard for organisations. It explains that most attacks are basic, and lists five technical controls that stop the most common ones.

  • GOV.UK: Cyber security breaches survey (opens in a new tab)

    The government's yearly survey of UK businesses and charities about the cyber problems they meet. The latest listed was published in April 2026. Useful for seeing how common attacks really are.

  • NCSC: Cyber attack (opens in a new tab)

    A topic page that gathers the NCSC's guidance on cyber attacks in one place.

  • NCSC: What you need to know about ransomware (opens in a new tab)

    What ransomware is, how to recognise it, and how to respond if you are a victim.

Check your understanding. A shop's till system runs old software with a known flaw, and a gang is scanning the internet for exactly that flaw. Which part is the threat, which is the vulnerability, and what is the risk?

Show a good answer

The threat is the gang scanning for the flaw. The vulnerability is the old software with a known flaw. The risk is the chance, and the harm, of the gang getting into the till system and the customer details behind it. The cheapest fix is to install the update.
