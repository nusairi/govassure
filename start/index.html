What you will learn

You do not need any technical background. By the end of this path you will be able to:

  • explain what cyber security is, in plain English, and the three ideas almost every security conversation uses
  • tell a threat, a weakness and a risk apart, and describe how attackers look for the easiest way in
  • protect your own accounts and devices using the advice the UK government's National Cyber Security Centre gives to everyone
  • spot a phishing message, and know how to report one
  • describe how information travels across a network, and why encryption and backups matter
  • see the kinds of work people do in security and where to explore a career

How to use this path

Open the steps in order, or jump to the one you need. Each page has a short looping explainer, a little reading, a quick thing to try, official UK guidance, and one or two video clips to watch when you have time. Tick “mark as done” on a step and it will show as done here. Ticks are saved on this device only. Nothing is sent to us.

The videos come from the free Google Cybersecurity Certificate playlist (opens in a new tab) on YouTube. They are long, so each link starts at the part that matches the step, and you can stop when you have the idea. They open in a new tab. We have written our own short summaries: under each video link you will find a “What this video covers” explainer and a simple graphic. The videos are made for a US audience, so where they mention US laws, job sites or figures, use the UK links on the page instead. The video makers have no connection with us.

If an animation feels like too much, use the pause control, or skip on and come back later. You will not be behind. The aim is a confident first pass, not a qualification.

Words you will see

A short glossary in plain English. You do not need to memorise it. Come back when a word turns up.

Asset
Anything of value to a person or organisation, such as a device, an account, photos or customer details.
Availability
The right people can get to the information or service when they need it.
Backup
A copy of important files kept somewhere separate, so you can recover them if the originals are lost.
Confidentiality
Information is only seen by the people who should see it.
Encryption
Scrambling information so that only someone with the right key can read it.
Integrity
Being able to trust that information is correct and has not been quietly changed.
Malware
Software designed to harm devices or networks, such as viruses, worms, trojans, ransomware and spyware.
Multi-factor authentication (MFA)
Proving who you are in two or more ways, such as a password plus a code on your phone. Also called 2-step verification.
Packet
A small unit of data that travels across a network, a bit like a letter with an address on the envelope.
Phishing
A message that pretends to be from someone you trust to get you to click, open or hand over details.
Ransomware
Malware that locks your data and demands payment to unlock it.
Risk
How likely something bad is to happen, and how serious it would be if it did.
Social engineering
Tricking people, rather than machines, into giving access or information.
Threat actor
Any person or group that poses a security risk, on purpose or by accident.
Update (patch)
A fix from the maker of software, often closing a security weakness.
Vulnerability
A weakness that a threat could use, such as out-of-date software or a weak password.
