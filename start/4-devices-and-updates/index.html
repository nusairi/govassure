Devices, updates and malware

Software has mistakes, and some mistakes let attackers in. When the maker finds one, they release an update. The moment a fix is public, attackers know where the weakness is on every device that has not applied it. That is why the NCSC describes installing updates as one of the most important, and quickest, things you can do to stay safe online.

Turn on automatic updates where you can. Check that your devices are still supported, because older devices eventually stop receiving updates. Delete apps and programs you no longer use, and wipe old devices properly before you sell or recycle them.

Malware is the general name for software designed to harm. The common routes in are phishing messages, infected files and out-of-date software, so updates plus a little care about what you open and install deal with most of it. If a device starts behaving strangely, the NCSC has a short page on what to do.

Try it for yourself

  • Open the settings on your phone or laptop, find software update, and turn on automatic updates. Then look through the apps on your phone and delete any you no longer use.

Videos to watch

These are clips from longer free videos. Each link starts at the right moment, and you are welcome to watch only that part.

  • YouTube · starts at 55:52 · about 4 minutes · part of a video that runs 1 hour 9 minutes

    Internet Networks & Network Security: OS hardening (opens in a new tab)

    A short, practical clip on making a computer's operating system harder to attack. The same habits apply to your own laptop or phone.

    What this video covers

    • Hardening means making a device or system tougher to attack, through routine jobs and one-off settings.
    • Install security updates (patches) as soon as they are released. Once a fix is public, attackers know exactly where the hole is on every device that has not been updated yet.
    • Keep a record of how a system should be set up, so that unexpected changes stand out.
    • Remove software you no longer use, and wipe old equipment properly before it is thrown away or passed on.
    • Use rules for passwords, such as a minimum length and a lock-out after repeated wrong tries, and add a second check wherever possible.

  • YouTube · starts at 1:36:30 · about 8 minutes · part of a video that runs 2 hours 6 minutes

    Cybersecurity Assets, Network Threats & Vulnerabilities: Malware (opens in a new tab)

    What malware is, the five kinds you will hear about most, and the warning signs of one newer kind that quietly uses your computer's power.

    What this video covers

    • Malware is software designed to harm devices or networks. It can spread through infected USB drives, phishing emails or the internet, and can let attackers take control without you knowing.
    • Five common kinds: a virus needs a person to open the infected file; a worm spreads across a network by itself; a trojan pretends to be a useful file or app; ransomware locks your data and demands payment; spyware secretly collects things like logins and PINs.
    • A newer kind, cryptojacking, secretly uses your computer's power to make cryptocurrency. Warning signs include slowness, heavy processor use, sudden crashes and a battery that drains fast.
    • Defences include clicking links only from sources you trust, using protective software and blockers, and staying informed. Detection tools help, but brand-new malware can slip past them.

What GOV.UK recommends

UK official advice on devices is practical and short. The Cyber Essentials scheme shows how organisations are expected to do the same things.

  • NCSC: Securing your devices (opens in a new tab)

    Points you to the manufacturers' own guides for Apple, Android, Samsung and Microsoft devices, and suggests checking your settings every few months and whenever you get a new device.

  • NCSC: Smart devices in the home (opens in a new tab)

    How to choose, set up, update and eventually get rid of connected items such as speakers, cameras and thermostats.

  • NCSC: Infected devices (opens in a new tab)

  • GOV.UK: Cyber Essentials scheme overview (opens in a new tab)

    The government-backed scheme for organisations. Its five controls cover firewalls, secure settings, security updates, who can access what, and malware protection.
