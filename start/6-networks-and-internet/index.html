Networks and the internet

The internet is a network of networks. When you open a web page, your device breaks the request into small packets. They travel through your router, your internet provider and the wider internet to the website, and the reply comes back the same way. Every device has an address, and a port number says which service on it should receive each packet.

Two practical ideas protect most people. First, encrypt what travels: look for https in web addresses, and think twice before using public Wi-Fi for anything sensitive. Second, keep things apart: for example, put smart gadgets on a guest network so a problem there does not reach your laptop.

At home, your router is the front door to your network. Change its default admin password, make sure Wi-Fi uses WPA2 or WPA3, keep its software up to date, and use a strong Wi-Fi password.

Try it for yourself

  • Look at your home Wi-Fi settings. What is the network called? Is it using WPA2 or WPA3? Is there a guest network you could use for smart devices and visitors? Your provider's help pages explain how to find these.

Videos to watch

These are clips from longer free videos. Each link starts at the right moment, and you are welcome to watch only that part.

  • YouTube · starts at 8:19 · about 9 minutes · part of a video that runs 1 hour 9 minutes

    Internet Networks & Network Security: Network communication (opens in a new tab)

    How information moves between devices, using letters and street addresses as the comparison. Some of the layer names are technical, so feel free to skim those.

    What this video covers

    • Information travels in small units called packets. Like a letter, each one has a destination address, a return address and the message itself.
    • Devices follow agreed sets of rules, called protocols. The main set splits the job into four layers, from the physical connection up to everyday services such as email and file transfer.
    • Ports work like flat numbers in a building: they tell the receiving device which service should get the data, for example email or secure web browsing.
    • An IP address says where a device is on the internet. A public address is assigned by your provider and shared by every device in one home or office; private addresses are only visible inside the local network. A device's MAC address identifies the hardware itself and helps a switch deliver packets.
    • Security staff watch how fast and how much data flows, because odd patterns can be a sign of an attack.

  • YouTube · starts at 37:22 · about 14 minutes · part of a video that runs 1 hour 9 minutes

    Internet Networks & Network Security: Network attack tactics (opens in a new tab)

    Why networks are attacked and the three main families of attack. The technical names (SYN, ICMP) are fine to skip: the ideas are flooding, eavesdropping and impersonating.

    What this video covers

    • Attacks can leak information, disrupt services, damage reputation and cost time and money. A 2014 breach at a US retail chain exposed card details of tens of millions of customers.
    • A denial-of-service attack floods a network or server so real users cannot get through. A distributed version uses many devices at once, and variants overwhelm a server with half-open connections, status requests or one oversized request.
    • Packet sniffing is watching data as it passes, like someone reading your post. An active version also changes the data, such as swapping a bank account number.
    • Protections against sniffing include a VPN, checking that sites start with https, and avoiding unprotected public Wi-Fi such as in cafés and airports.
    • Spoofing means faking where data comes from to get past checks. It includes sitting in the middle of a connection, replaying a captured message later, and floods that use a stolen address. Encryption is the main defence.

Check your understanding. You are in a café and need to check your bank balance. What is the safer choice, and why?

Show a good answer

Use your phone's mobile data rather than the café's Wi-Fi. Anyone can set up a hotspot, and on an unprotected network someone could watch your traffic. If you must use public Wi-Fi, use a reputable VPN and look for https.
