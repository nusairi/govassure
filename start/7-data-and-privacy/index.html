Data, encryption and privacy

Personal data is anything that can point to a person: a name, address, email, photos, health or money details. UK data protection law controls how organisations may use it, and gives you rights over your own data. Encryption is one of the main tools for protecting it, because information that is scrambled is useless without the key.

Backups protect against loss. A backup is a copy of your important files stored somewhere separate. The NCSC points out that setting this up takes little time and can often run automatically, and could save you a lot of stress.

When an organisation that holds your details has a problem, criminals often use the news to send convincing scam messages. The NCSC's guidance on data breaches for individuals explains what to watch for and what to do.

Try it for yourself

  • Check that your phone and computer back up your photos and documents automatically, and find out where the copy is kept. Then look at the privacy settings on one social media account.

Videos to watch

These are clips from longer free videos. Each link starts at the right moment, and you are welcome to watch only that part.

  • YouTube · starts at 32:05 · about 13 minutes · part of a video that runs 2 hours 6 minutes

    Cybersecurity Assets, Network Threats & Vulnerabilities: Encryption methods (opens in a new tab)

    What personal data is and the main tool for keeping it private. The later parts are technical; the first few minutes and the lock-and-key comparisons are the best place to stop if it gets heavy.

    What this video covers

    • Personally identifiable information (PII) is anything that can point to a person, such as a name, medical or financial details, photos, emails or fingerprints. Keeping it private online is hard and takes the right controls.
    • Encryption scrambles readable information into unreadable text and decryption turns it back using a key. An ancient letter-shifting trick shows the idea, and also why one easily guessed or stolen key is a weakness.
    • Modern systems use two kinds. With asymmetric encryption there is a public key anyone can use to lock something and a private key only the owner has to unlock it, like a letter box with a slot. Symmetric encryption uses one shared secret key and is faster. Chat apps often use the first to start a conversation and the second to carry on.
    • Digital certificates, issued by trusted organisations, act like online ID badges that show a website is who it says it is.
    • Hashing makes a one-way fingerprint of a file. Any change gives a different fingerprint, which is how people check a file has not been altered.

  • YouTube · starts at 6:26 · about 3 minutes · part of a video that runs 1 hour 30 minutes

    How To Prepare For Your Cybersecurity Career: Your impact on data protection (opens in a new tab)

    A short reminder that protecting data is a team effort and that speaking up early is part of the job. Useful for anyone who handles other people's information at work.

    What this video covers

    • Protecting data is a team effort: some people focus on financial data, some on usernames and passwords, some on supplier security and some on staff personal details.
    • Customers trust organisations to protect card numbers, emails, usernames and passwords, so understanding how important the data is is part of having a security mindset.
    • An event that leads to a data breach is an incident; one that is resolved before any breach is not. Either way it is better to be careful and report than to assume it is fine.
    • Small things matter too: someone installing an unapproved app on a work device should be raised, because some apps contain weaknesses. No issue is too small to mention.

Check your understanding. Why is a backup about more than protecting against theft?

Show a good answer

Data can be lost in many ways: a dropped or broken device, an accident, or ransomware that locks your files. A backup, kept separately from the device, lets you recover. Protection against theft is only one reason.
