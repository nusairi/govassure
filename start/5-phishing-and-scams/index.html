Phishing and social engineering

Phishing is a message that pretends to be from someone you trust, to get you to click a link, open a file or hand over details. It arrives by email, text message, phone call or social media. The NCSC says scams are getting smarter: bad spelling is no longer a reliable clue, and some even fool experts.

The NCSC lists tell-tale signs. Is the message claiming to come from someone in authority? Does it create urgency, with a short deadline or a threat? Does it stir up emotion such as panic, fear, hope or curiosity? Does it offer something scarce? Does it fit something you are expecting, or does it piggy-back on current events?

If you have doubts, stop and contact the organisation directly, using details from its official website or a bill or card, not the ones in the message. Then report it. You can forward scam texts for free to 7726 if your phone provider is part of the scheme, and the NCSC's pages explain how to report scam emails, calls, websites and adverts.

Try it for yourself

  • Next time a message makes you feel rushed or worried, stop and count to ten. Look for the signs above. Then check by opening the organisation's real website or app yourself, rather than using the link.

    Cybersecurity Assets, Network Threats & Vulnerabilities: Social engineering and phishing (opens in a new tab)

    How attackers use manipulation instead of clever code, and the most common form, phishing. The final section on mail filters is aimed at organisations; the rest is useful for everyone.

    What this video covers

    • Social engineering is manipulation that gets people to break normal security rules on an attacker's behalf. It can happen online, in person or by phone, and can take seconds or months.
    • Typical stages: prepare by researching the target, build trust by pretending to be someone else, persuade the person to hand over information, then disappear.
    • Phishing is the most common form. A phishing kit can include infected attachments, fake forms that ask for sensitive details, and links to look-alike websites.
    • Smishing is the same trick by text message, and vishing is by phone, for example a caller claiming there is a problem with your account.
    • Defences include clear rules, training, mail filters, and above all sharing what you learn so that other people recognise the signs. Attackers rely on curiosity and a wish to help.

Show a good answer

It may arrive when you are not expecting a parcel, it asks you to act quickly, and it wants you to pay through a link. Instead, do not tap the link. Check with the courier or the shop through their official app or website that you already use, and forward the text to 7726 to report it.
